A collection of YouTube thumbnails designed for Jalso, turning conversations, films, people and ideas into visuals that have to make sense in a second.

> **“You get a few seconds to make someone stop scrolling.”**

_The Thumbnail Game_ is a collection of YouTube thumbnail designs I created while working with **Jalso Production House**. What started as one of my responsibilities within the production team became a regular exercise in understanding how much information a single image can carry, and how quickly it has to communicate.

These were not designs that had days to develop. Most thumbnails were made within **30–45 minutes**, often around ongoing productions and changing requirements. The challenge was to understand the subject quickly, identify the strongest hook, find the right image, build a clear hierarchy and make the whole thing work at a glance.

## **The Context**

During my time at Jalso, I worked across several roles: **Producer, Writer, Researcher, Cinematographer, Editor, and Designer**.

Thumbnail design became one of the smaller but surprisingly demanding parts of that work.

The videos covered a wide range of subjects: Gujarati cinema, actors, artists, spirituality, mythology, culture, social issues, conversations and current topics. Each one needed a different visual treatment. There was no single template that could solve everything.

A film discussion might need expressive faces and a strong title. A cultural conversation might need a more editorial treatment. A spiritual topic might rely on symbolism and atmosphere. Sometimes the most important thing was simply making the guest and the central question immediately recognisable.

## **The Approach**

The first question was always: **what is the one thing someone should understand from this thumbnail?**

From there, I would work quickly with whatever material was available: photographs from the shoot, portraits, film stills, screenshots, graphic elements and the topic itself.

The designs rely heavily on **large type, faces, colour contrast, cut-outs, layering and visual hierarchy**. Gujarati and English typography were often mixed depending on the audience and subject.

The goal wasn't to make every thumbnail look beautiful in isolation.

The goal was to make it **clear, recognisable and difficult to ignore when surrounded by dozens of other videos.**

> **“A thumbnail isn't the whole story. It's the reason someone decides to hear the story.”**

## **02 - Finding the Hook**

![Another example for thumbnails](/uploads/2.png)

As the subjects became more varied, the challenge became less about making a consistent visual style and more about finding the **specific hook of each episode**.

For example, a film discussion could be built around the film itself, while a conversation about spirituality might need a symbolic visual language. The thumbnail had to tell the viewer what kind of conversation they were about to enter without requiring them to read the entire title.

## **03** - **Designing at Speed**

![Quickly made thumbnails](/uploads/3.png)

These are examples of the quick-turnaround work that became part of the production process.

With roughly **30–45 minutes per thumbnail**, there wasn't much room for endless experimentation. Decisions had to happen quickly, which photograph to use, what to remove, what to enlarge, where the headline should sit, and what colour would make it visible immediately.

That constraint became part of the exercise.

Instead of trying to perfect every small detail, I learned to recognise what actually matters at thumbnail size.

## **What I Learned**

Working on thumbnails taught me something quite different from longer design projects.

A poster can ask someone to spend a few minutes looking at it. A YouTube thumbnail gets something closer to a **split second**.

That meant learning to prioritise.

**One strong image over five weak ones.**
**One clear idea over too much information.**
**Hierarchy before decoration.**
**Recognition before detail.**

The speed also became valuable. Designing repeatedly under real production conditions taught me to make decisions without getting overly attached to them — something that has carried into my other design and filmmaking work.

## **Working with Jalso**

These thumbnails are part of the larger body of work I created during my time at **Jalso Production House**, where I worked from **July 2024 to November 2025**.

Alongside thumbnail and graphic design, I worked across **production, writing, research, cinematography and editing** for Jalso's podcasts, documentaries, music videos and other productions.

The thumbnails were a small part of that larger role, but they gave me a different understanding of visual communication: **how to compress an entire conversation into one image that has to earn the viewer's attention.**
