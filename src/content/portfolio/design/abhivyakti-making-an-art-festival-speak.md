# **Abhivyakti - Making an Arts Festival Speak**

> **“An arts festival is not only what happens on stage. It is everything that makes people want to walk in.”**

Abhivyakti - The City Arts Project is a multidisciplinary arts initiative bringing together different forms of artistic expression, including art, music, dance, theatre and literature. This project involved designing communication around the festival and finding ways to make a large amount of information feel accessible, recognisable and connected to the larger identity of Abhivyakti.

My work explored how the identity could live beyond a single poster or logo - across announcements, artist calls, curator introductions, event information, interactive posts and other digital communication.

## **The Brief**

The challenge was to communicate a festival with many different voices without making every piece of communication feel like a completely different project.

There were several kinds of things that needed to be communicated: the festival itself, different artistic disciplines, open calls, artists and curators, events, interactive content and information for audiences.

The visual identity already had a distinctive character, so the work was less about inventing something from scratch and more about understanding the existing language and finding ways to stretch it across different situations.

The Abhivyakti identity shown across the communication combines the festival name with a visual system built around art, music, dance, theatre and literature.

## **Building the Language**

One of the things I enjoyed about the project was figuring out how much variety could exist inside the same visual system.

Different posts could have completely different subjects and colours while still feeling like they belonged to Abhivyakti.

The communication used bold colour fields, large typography, the Abhivyakti identity, illustrated elements, and photography depending on what each post needed to communicate.

The five discipline cards became one of the clearest examples of this approach - each discipline gets its own colour and visual treatment, but the overall structure keeps them connected.

## **Social Media Communication**

A large part of the work was translating the festival into a language that could work quickly on social media.

Posts such as **“Calling All Dancers,” “Calling All Visual Artists,” “Music Curator,” “Theatre Curator,”** and artist application announcements had very different messages, but they needed to work as part of the same visual world.

![](/uploads/Screenshot_20260729-193656.png)

The challenge was to make the important information immediately visible - who the post was for, what was happening, what action was expected, and where the viewer needed to go next.

At the same time, I didn't want the communication to become purely informational. The colour, scale of type and use of imagery were used to give each post some energy of its own.

## **Designing for Different Voices**

Abhivyakti isn't one kind of art.

It brings together different disciplines, artists and audiences, and the communication had to reflect that variety.

Some pieces were loud and direct. Some were more editorial. Some relied on portraits of people, while others used illustrations, patterns or graphic compositions.

This became an interesting design exercise because consistency didn't mean making everything look identical.

For me, the more useful question was:

**How different can the pieces become before they stop feeling like the same festival?**

## **Making Information Interactive**

Not everything had to be an announcement.

One of the posts asked the audience **“What elements do you see?”**, turning the visual identity itself into something to interact with rather than simply something to look at.

![Different element designs](/uploads/Screenshot_20260729-193340.png)

I liked this part of the project because it changed the relationship between the identity and the audience. Instead of only telling people about the festival, the design could also give them something to notice, interpret, and respond to.

## **Working Within an Existing Identity**

The project taught me that designing for an established cultural platform is very different from designing a completely personal project.

There are already things that have to remain recognisable: the name, identity, tone and relationship with the audience.

The interesting part is finding the space around those fixed elements where you can still experiment.

The Abhivyakti identity became the constant, while colour, imagery, composition, typography and content changed according to what each communication piece needed.

## **The Work**

The collection brings together different parts of the communication work: festival identity applications, discipline-based graphics, artist and curator communication, calls for participation, event information and interactive social content.

Rather than treating each piece as an isolated graphic, I approached them as parts of one larger visual conversation.

## **What I Took From It**

The biggest lesson from Abhivyakti was that **a visual identity is only as useful as its ability to adapt.**

A good system shouldn't tell you exactly what every poster has to look like. It should give you enough structure to make many different things feel like they belong together.

Working on Abhivyakti allowed me to think about design beyond individual compositions - about identity, audience, information, culture, and how a visual language behaves when it has to live across an entire project.
